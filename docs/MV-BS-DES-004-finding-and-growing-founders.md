# MV-BS-DES-004 — Finding and growing transformational founders: research synthesis

**Business Simulator · Masika Ventures**

**Status:** Ideation for owner review. Not accepted. No code change.

**Date:** 2026-09-30

**Extends:** [P-002](../memory/DECISIONS.md) (tranche pyramid), [P-003](../memory/DECISIONS.md)
(funnel and game), [D-036](../memory/DECISIONS.md), [D-038](../memory/DECISIONS.md) and
[MV-BS-DES-003](./MV-BS-DES-003-business-plan-questions.md) (plan questions).

**Question from the owner (2026-09-30):** How can the programme find the 0.1–1% of people
who can build firms of 100 to 1,000 people, as cheaply as possible? How can it make sure
that only those people reach the USD 10,000 and USD 50,000 stages? What should
participants learn? How can the programme help able people succeed and not drop out?

---

## 1. Summary

1. **You cannot pick the 0.1% at entry.** Firms that grow this large are very rare. Every
   known method predicts growth poorly from one observation. The funnel must let people
   **show** their ability over time, through independent observations that get more
   difficult to fake at each stage.
2. **Measure the slope, not the level.** A person's current sales, schooling and practices
   mostly show the opportunity they had. How fast they learn, adapt and improve shows
   ability. The "diamond in the rough" has a low level and a steep slope.
3. **Observe behaviour. Do not ask for self-description.** Plan quality, pitches and
   personality questionnaires predict poorly. Kept commitments, records, hires and
   calibrated forecasts predict better and are more difficult to fake.
4. **The skill that separates a 5-person firm from a 500-person firm is organisation:**
   delegation, hiring people from outside the family, and using specialists. The later
   gates must test this directly.
5. **Support is also selection.** Offer mentors, peer groups and back-office services.
   Do not make them mandatory (D-038). How a person uses them is evidence.
6. **Protect able people from avoidable drop-out.** Common causes are cash shocks, household
   claims on business money, isolation and a heavy reporting load. Each has a low-cost fix
   with evidence behind it.

## 2. The problem: rare outcomes and weak prediction

| Fact | Source | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| In India, surviving plants barely grow with age; employment rises about 1% a year or less. In the US it rises about 5% a year. | Hsieh & Klenow 2014 | Growth to hundreds of workers is very rare. Expect almost all participants not to reach it. |
| Most microentrepreneurs run a business from necessity. Few want to hire. | Jayachandran 2021 | Most participants are livelihood firms. That is legitimate, and they are not the target of the top gates. |
| Judges' plan scores did not predict outcomes. All methods, including machine learning, had low predictive power. | McKenzie & Sansone 2019 | One snapshot cannot find the tail. |
| If you select the highest of many uncertain estimates, the chosen option is on average worse than its estimate. | Smith & Winkler 2006 (optimizer's curse) | A top score at one stage is partly luck. Confirm it with a new, independent observation before a larger grant. |
| Top junior athletes and adult world-class athletes are largely different people. World-class adults progressed more slowly at first. | Güllich, Macnamara & Hambrick 2022 | Do not permanently exclude slow starters. Allow re-entry (P-002). |
| Children from the top 1% of incomes were 10 times more likely to become inventors. Exposure, not ability, explains much of the gap. | Bell, Chetty et al. 2019 | Talent exists where opportunity does not. Recruit widely, outside existing networks. |
| Asset transfers moved households above a threshold, and they then grew out of poverty. Households below it fell back. | Balboni et al. 2022 | Low current performance can be a trap, not low ability. Size grants to cross a named threshold (P-002). |

## 3. What predicts, and what does not

Strength: **A** = several strong studies; **B** = one strong study or indirect evidence;
**C** = weak or no outcome evidence.

| Signal | Evidence | Strength | How to observe cheaply |
|---|---|---|---|
| Learning ability | An ability index predicted growth (Fafchamps & Woodruff 2017). Learning aptitude predicted entry into incorporated self-employment (Levine & Rubinstein 2017). General ability predicted CEO performance (Kaplan, Klebanov & Sorensen 2012). | A | Improvement across repeated game decisions after feedback. Do not use school-based tests; they measure past opportunity. |
| Execution and proactivity | Execution skills predicted CEO success more than interpersonal skills; boards over-weighted interpersonal skills (Kaplan et al. 2012). Task-specific traits (proactivity, need for achievement, self-efficacy) correlate about 0.25 with success; broad Big Five traits about 0.03 (Rauch & Frese 2007). | A | A dated action that happened (DES-003 Q9). Time from decision to action. |
| Business practices | They predict profit, survival and growth in seven countries (McKenzie & Woodruff 2017). Many firms do not know good practices exist (Bloom et al. 2013). | A | Practices checklist at application and in each report. **The change is the signal.** |
| Existing traction | Businesses that already existed gained from credit; others did not (Banerjee et al. 2019). Founders with a previous success succeed more often again (Gompers et al. 2010). | A | Trading now, since when, weekly sales, earlier tests. |
| Self-selection | Farmers who chose to borrow had high returns; others near zero (Beaman et al. 2023). A small application cost improved targeting; a larger one did not help more (Alatas et al. 2016). | A | Completing the game and the short plan is enough effort. Do not add heavy barriers. |
| Calibrated forecasting and updating | Forecast error in both directions went with lower profit (Tanaka et al. 2020, large firms). Hypothesis testing improved performance and ended weak ideas sooner (Camuffo et al. 2020, 2024). | B | Forecast range against the actual result; explanation of the gap (D-036). |
| Delegation and organisation | Weak delegation keeps firms small and explains about 11% of the income gap between the US and India (Akcigit, Alp & Peters 2021). Owners who could monitor better delegated more (Bloom et al. 2013). | B | Has anyone from outside the family worked for pay? Does the business run when the owner is away? |
| Peer knowledge | Community rankings found owners with 23–35% monthly returns; the average was 11%. Rankings needed incentive design (Hussam, Rigol & Roth 2022). | B | Cohort peer ranking at the USD 10,000 gate, designed to resist bias. |
| Early growth choices | Choices at founding (for example, registration) predicted large growth outcomes; 75% of them were in the top 5% of predicted quality (Guzman & Stern, US). | B | Registration, separate business account, formal supplier or customer contracts. Evidence is from the US. |
| Structured interview | The best single predictor of job performance (validity 0.42). Work samples 0.33; cognitive tests 0.31 (Sackett et al. 2022). | A (employment) | The game is a work sample. At the USD 10,000 gate, use a structured interview with fixed questions and anchored ratings. |
| Psychometric test | Allowed lending to people without credit history with no increase in default (Arráiz, Bruhn & Stucchi, Peru). | B | Possible research measure at stage 0. People can learn to game it when stakes are high. |
| Business-plan quality or pitch | Did not predict (McKenzie & Sansone 2019). | Negative | Do not use to rank. |
| Grit | Correlates 0.84 with conscientiousness; adds little (Credé et al. 2017). | C | Do not measure. |
| Growth-mindset scales | Very weak link to achievement; interventions d ≈ 0.08 (Sisk et al. 2018). | C | Do not measure. Observe updating instead. |
| Coachability questionnaire | Affects investor opinions; little evidence on outcomes (Ciuchta et al. 2018). | C | Observe uptake of feedback instead: the change in practices after advice. |
| "Smart and illicit" | Future entrepreneurs combined high learning aptitude with rule-breaking in youth (Levine & Rubinstein 2017, US). | B | Warning only: do not score conformity. Keep the integrity rule narrow (§8). |

## 4. The funnel, stage by stage

Each stage adds a **different** source of evidence. Decisions use the evidence from **all**
earlier stages, not only the last one.

| Stage | Cost per person | New evidence | Decision | Support offered |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **0 — Game** (thousands) | Near zero to the programme | Work sample: learning over repeated decisions, recovery after shocks, forecast calibration in the game, use of records, completion and return visits. | Permissive. The owner has deferred the entry rule. | Game learning. Role-model stories (§6). |
| **1 — USD 1,000** (many) | USD 1,000 + short review | DES-003 plan: baseline, commitments, practices. Then 6 months of real behaviour: dated action done, records kept, practices change, forecast against actual, how the money was used. | **Recommended:** a minimum quality check (complete, specific, consistent), then a lottery among those who pass. The random part also gives the comparison group (Q-034). | Peer group, optional mentor, record summaries (§6, §7). |
| **2 — USD 10,000** (few) | Grant + interview + verification | Structured interview. Independent checks: records photos, a supplier or customer call, a visit. Cohort peer ranking. Delegation: the business runs while the owner is away. | Strict. Cumulative evidence. A human reviewer decides with reasons. | Back-office outsourcing or a hired specialist (Anderson & McKenzie 2021). Mentor. |
| **3 — USD 50,000** (very few) | Grant + deep review | Financial statements from records; a lender's decision (D-038). Non-family manager in place. A second bottleneck named, costed and removed. | Strictest. Third-party evidence. | Lender introductions, specialist hires. |

**Why a lottery at stage 1:** judged ranking at this level did not beat simple facts
(McKenzie & Sansone 2019). A USD 1,000 false positive is cheap; a lost founder is expensive
(P-002). A lottery above a minimum check costs no reviewer time, is fair, and makes the
pilot's evidence valid.

**Evidence for staged grants:** a first US R&D grant roughly doubled the chance of later
venture capital, most for financially constrained firms (Howell 2017). In Start-Up Chile,
cash and co-working space alone had no effect; the added school for the top-ranked firms
increased capital raised three to six times (Gonzalez-Uribe & Leatherbee 2018). Money is
necessary but not enough; the support at the upper stages matters.

**Why independent evidence at each gate:** a high score at one stage is partly luck
(optimizer's curse). A person who is high again on a **different** measure is much more
likely to be truly high.

## 5. What participants should learn

These are both **linked to growth** in the research and **teachable**. The last column
shows where the current game already teaches them.

| # | Capability | Evidence | Game today |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Keep records **and look at simple summaries** of them | Practices predict growth (McKenzie & Woodruff 2017). Farmers did not learn from their own data until given a summary that pointed at the important dimension (Hanna, Mullainathan & Schwartzstein 2014). | Notebook and weekly "why". Add: point at one dimension each time. |
| 2 | Keep business money separate from household money | Rule-of-thumb training worked; standard accounting training did not (Drexler et al. 2014). Household claims absorb capital (Bernhardt et al. 2019). | Household payments exist. |
| 3 | Know the cost of one item and the margin | Practices checklist item. | Partly. |
| 4 | Forecast, test small, update | Camuffo et al. 2020; D-036. | Why prompts; no committed forecast yet (P-003 §4). |
| 5 | Act before problems: dated personal plans | Personal-initiative training +30% profit (Campos et al. 2017). Soft-skills training raised profit 35% (Chioda et al., Uganda). | Standing plans. |
| 6 | **Delegate: hire, train, check, step away** | Akcigit et al. 2021; Bloom et al. 2013. | One helper and one away week. **The largest gap for the target outcome.** |
| 7 | Use specialists instead of doing everything | Outsourcing and insourcing did better than training, at half the cost of consulting (Anderson & McKenzie 2021). | Not present. |
| 8 | Build supplier, customer and peer networks | Monthly peer meetings raised revenue 8.1%, and the effect lasted (Cai & Szeidl 2018). | School and kiosk orders. |
| 9 | Cash versus profit, working capital, bankability | D-038; P-003 §5. | Cash, sales and profit are separate. |

**On records:** do not require records at entry. Many people have never seen what records
do (Bloom et al. 2013), so a requirement would exclude them. Instead, **measure whether a
person starts keeping records after the game shows their value.** That change is direct
evidence of learning.

## 6. Low-cost additions with evidence

- **Role-model stories.** A one-hour documentary about local people who succeeded raised
  aspirations and increased investment five years later (Bernard, Dercon, Orkin & Taffesse,
  Ethiopia). A short local video costs little.
- **Monthly peer groups of non-competitors** (Cai & Szeidl 2018). Let people join with a
  friend; women trained with a friend increased business activity, while those trained alone
  did not (Field et al. 2016).
- **A mentor who owned a local business in the same sector.** Mentoring raised profit about 20%.
  The effect faded when mentoring stopped (Brooks et al. 2018). There is suggestive evidence that
  a trainer with a shared identity changes mindset where others do not (Alibhai et al. 2019). Keep contact
  regular and continuous.
- **Record summaries returned to the participant:** one number that matters, each month.

## 7. Why able people drop out, and what to do

| Cause | Evidence | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Cash shocks and financial worry | Early pay reduced worry and increased output (Kaur et al. 2025). | Pay on fixed, published dates. Make the first payment fast. Never make participants chase money. |
| Household claims on business money | Bernhardt et al. 2019; Fafchamps et al. 2014. | Separate account. Offer payment to the named supplier (see §8, C1). |
| Isolation | Field et al. 2016; Cai & Szeidl 2018. | Cohort peer groups; join with a friend. |
| Support stops | Brooks et al. 2018. | Continuous light contact, not short courses. |
| Owner overload | Akcigit et al. 2021; Anderson & McKenzie 2021. | Back-office outsourcing (D-038). |
| Reporting load and data cost | AGENTS.md §3; D-037. | Short reports, photos compressed on the phone, offline queue (already designed). |
| Fear of failure being punished | D-036: honest failure is information. | Reward honest explanation of a miss. Allow re-entry to a later cohort (P-002). |

## 8. Conflicts with existing decisions

- **C1 — Restricted grants.** D-038 says "restricted grants are nonsense." Research shows
  that cash leaked from women's businesses and in-kind grants did not (Fafchamps et al.
  2014). **Proposal:** keep grants unrestricted, but make the default payment go to the
  named supplier for the named purchase. The participant can choose cash. Record the choice.
- **C2 — No ranking.** ADR-0004 forbids scores and ranks in the game record. Peer ranking
  and structured interview ratings at the USD 10,000 gate are programme evidence, not
  game-record content. They need their own ADR.
- **C3 — Integrity rule.** ADR-0010 penalises AI-written answers. Keep integrity about
  fabricated evidence and outsourced reasoning. Do not penalise informality, unconventional
  choices or rule-bending in the business itself (Levine & Rubinstein 2017).
- **C4 — What the game can show.** The game is a food stall. It can show learning,
  execution and calibration. It cannot show the ability to build a 500-person organisation.
  Do not over-interpret stage 0. The later gates must test organisation directly.

## 9. Limits

- No study validates a method for finding the top 0.1% of microentrepreneurs in East
  Africa. Most evidence is about average effects, not the extreme tail.
- A pilot of 50 grants cannot validate tail selection. It can validate the mechanics and
  early signals. Keep records for everyone, including a random slice (Q-034).
- Several predictors come from other contexts: CEOs, US start-ups, employment selection,
  sport. They are analogies, not proof.
- Findings in this document are from abstracts and author summaries. Check exact figures
  before external use.

## 10. Decisions for the owner

1. Adopt §1 as the design basis for the funnel.
2. Stage 1: minimum check plus a lottery, or a ranked selection?
3. Add the delegation, hiring and registration questions to DES-003 (§11).
4. C1: default payment to the named supplier, with a cash option?
5. USD 10,000 gate: structured interview, independent verification and peer ranking
   (needs an ADR).
6. Support: peer groups, local mentors and back-office outsourcing, offered and not required.
7. Game priority: delegation and organisation, and record summaries that point at one
   dimension.

Add to section A of DES-003:

  Yes / No; how many.

  runs it / a worker runs it.
