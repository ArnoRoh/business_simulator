# Finding export entrepreneurs who would otherwise be missed

*One-page concept note. The long version, with the cost model, is
[`concept-note.html`](./concept-note.html).*

---

## The claim

One of the binding constraints on export manufacturing in Tanzania, Ethiopia and
countries like them is **finding the people who can found and run the companies**. That
ability is rare, but it is not distributed the way capital and contacts are. People who
have it never get near the starting line, because the ways founders are normally found
— who you know, who can afford to stop earning, who can write a fundable-looking plan —
select for wealth and connection first and ability second.

**If those people can be found cheaply and at volume, funding them is the cheapest
economic growth available.** The question the design answers is narrow: *how do you take
someone driving a taxi or running a shop and get them to a company a commercial bank
will lend millions in working capital to?*

## Why the usual filters do not work

Business plans and pitch scores predict later performance poorly. So do degrees,
charisma and hardship stories. Any filter built on how well someone *describes* a
business rewards those who already had the education and the coaching — the exact filter
we are trying to get around. What predicts better is **observed execution**: what
someone actually did, repeatedly, under constraint. So we watch behaviour instead of
reading proposals.

## The funnel

| Stage | What happens | Gate | Money |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0** | A free phone game: run a stall, a bakery, a factory, an export business — eighty decisions with real consequences | Finished it, and the record shows how they decided | — |
| **1** | A short plan submitted through a portal, written as **dated numeric predictions** about their own real firm. AI-graded | The plan applies what the game taught, and its claims are specific enough to be wrong | **~USD 1,000** |
| **2** | Six to twelve months later: what actually happened, next to what they predicted | What did they spend it on, can they explain the gap, do they keep records | ~USD 10,000 |
| **3** | A last tranche, plus back-office and management support | Same test, bigger numbers | Larger again |

Stage 1 is not really a competition. It is **friction** — something that costs effort
to get through. The real test is stage 2: *why did my plan not work?*

**Exit: a bankable firm.** Credit is not scarce in these markets; being creditworthy
is. The programme carries a firm across that threshold and hands it to commercial
lenders, which gives a success measure nobody can talk their way past.

## The one idea that holds it together

**A plan is a forecast, not a proposal.** We do not grade the writing. We record what
the applicant says will happen, with dates and numbers, and we come back. A language
model can write a fluent plan; it cannot make the numbers come true. And the best
signal is not whether they hit the forecast — it is whether they can say precisely why
they missed it. Grading accuracy alone would reward sandbagging and luck.

The game teaches exactly this: predict, reveal, diagnose, eighty times. Stages 1 and 2
are the same move performed on a real business.

## Small money first, on purpose

Money given too early and too generously makes founders believe capital is cheap: they
stop being hungry and over-value businesses with no traction. Some capable people will
also just take it and meet a more urgent need — rational, and informative. So the first
tranche is small, roughly a few months of living costs, and every step up is earned by
demonstrated traction at a **fixed amount per stage**, so founders are compared with
each other rather than with their own ask.

## What it costs

The pilot is **USD 50,000 in grants — 50 grants of 1,000** — with roughly the same again
in running costs. Stage 0 costs nothing per extra learner: a static offline-capable web
app, plans judged by an AI. Reaching one finisher costs about **USD 22**, and the model
puts the pilot at **2.7× the benefit of giving the same money away as cash** on an
assumed 6% rate of transformational firms among those funded. *Modelled figures on
stated assumptions, not results — nothing here has been measured yet.*

## The hard part, named

**Filling the top of the funnel.** The design only works at volume, and the channels
that are easiest to reach — professional networks, programme alumni — select for the
same people the usual filters already find. Reaching taxi drivers and shopkeepers needs
in-country partners with existing audiences, online learning platforms that can hand the
game to their users as a free extra, and social media not aimed at career professionals.
This is the largest uncontrolled filter in the system and the least solved.

## What the pilot is really buying

Not 50 firms. **Evidence about whether the filter works** — and, if it does, a
**playbook other funders can copy**, because the money that runs competitions like this
already exists and is mostly spent badly. Six months after stage 0 watched people decide
in a simulation, stage 2 watches the same people run real businesses: the first honest
test of whether observed play predicts observed execution.

The risk we take seriously: a pilot that only funds people the filter liked cannot tell
you whether the filter was right. Part of the grant budget has to go to applicants the
filter did not pick.

## Where it stands

The game is built, playable in English and Kiswahili, and live. The portal, the AI
judge and the record that carries an applicant from stage 0 to stage 2 are designed and
not yet built.
