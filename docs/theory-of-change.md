# Theory of change

> **Status:** first draft, unreviewed. This document states the causal chain the project
> assumes, and — more importantly — where that chain is weakest.

A theory of change that only lists the steps is marketing. This one names the
assumption behind each link and how it could fail, because the project's own thesis is
that development programmes overstate their causal claims.

---

## The chain

```
  Learner plays the simulation
            │
            ▼
  [1] Learner makes decisions under realistic constraint
            │
            ├──────────────► [A] Learning pathway
            │                     Learner sees consequences, updates,
            │                     applies to their own firm
            │
            └──────────────► [B] Selection pathway
                                  Behavioural record shows programme staff
                                  what the candidate actually does
            │
            ▼
  [2] Better-matched support reaches better-identified firms
            │
            ▼
  [3] More firms cross organisational thresholds
            │
            ▼
  [4] Higher value added per worker; durable formal employment
```

The two pathways are independent. **[A] can succeed while [B] fails**, and that outcome
is still worth having — it just makes this a teaching tool rather than a selection
instrument. Design accordingly: do not let the selection ambition compromise the
teaching, since teaching is the more certain of the two.

---

## Link by link

### [1] Play produces decisions that resemble real ones

**Assumption:** Decisions made in a simulation, with no real money at stake, resemble
those a person would make in their own business.

**Why it might hold:** The constraints can be made real — cash runs out, suppliers fail
to deliver, a certification takes four months and costs more than budgeted. Constraint,
not stakes, drives much of decision quality.

**How it fails:** Players optimise for the game. Someone who would never risk their own
capital takes a flier because losing costs nothing. Someone who reads the scoring
guesses what the tool wants and performs it.

**Mitigation:** Do not publish the scoring model to learners. Vary scenarios so pattern
matching does not pay. Score process and adaptation rather than outcome, so there is no
single "winning line" to memorise.

**Confidence: moderate.** This is the assumption most within our control.

### [A] Learning pathway: consequence changes behaviour

**Assumption:** Seeing a decision play out changes what someone does in their own firm.

**Why it might hold:** Consequence-based learning has a reasonable general evidence
base, and the specific failure modes here — underestimating formalisation cost,
under-pricing, not delegating, treating working capital as growth capital — are exactly
the sort of thing a simulation can make visible cheaply.

**How it fails:** Classic transfer problem. The learner understands it inside the
simulation and does not connect it to their own situation. Or they connect it and cannot
act, because the constraint is capital, not knowledge.

**Mitigation:** Require the learner to name their own binding constraint, in their own
words, at defined points. Ground scenarios in recognisable local value chains so
transfer is short.

**Confidence: moderate.** The knowledge-is-not-the-constraint objection is real and
partly unanswerable — if the problem is capital, teaching does not solve it.

### [B] Selection pathway: the record carries usable signal

**Assumption:** Observed in-simulation behaviour distinguishes candidates in a way that
predicts real performance better than a business plan does.

**Why it might hold:** The bar is low. Plan scores predict poorly; a modest correlation
would be an improvement. And this observes behaviour over many decisions rather than a
single document.

**How it fails:** In-simulation behaviour may measure familiarity with games, screen
literacy, or free time — not entrepreneurial capability. That would systematically
disadvantage exactly the experienced operators the model says to favour: an older
mechanic running a real workshop may perform worse than a young graduate who is good
at apps.

**Mitigation:** None adequate yet. This must be validated externally, not asserted.
[Q-003](../memory/OPEN_QUESTIONS.md) tracks it, and
[`assessment.md`](./assessment.md) forbids claiming predictive validity until it
exists.

**Confidence: low, and honestly so.** This is the weakest link and the most important
one. It is also the one a funder will most want us to overstate.

### [2] Better-identified firms receive better-matched support

**Assumption:** Programmes will change how they select if given a better signal.

**How it fails:** Institutional inertia. Selection processes are embedded in
procurement rules, board approvals and funder reporting. A better signal that no
process can ingest changes nothing.

**Mitigation:** [Q-002](../memory/OPEN_QUESTIONS.md) — find the actual consumer early
and design the output to fit what they can use. A record nobody can act on is a
research artefact.

**Confidence: low.** Almost entirely outside our control.

### [3] More firms cross organisational thresholds

**Assumption:** Better selection plus bottleneck-targeted support produces more firms
that delegate management, invest in capability, and grow beyond the founder.

**How it fails:** The binding constraint may be none of the things we affect — it may be
capital markets, electricity, road access, or the absence of anchor buyers. Better
selection of candidates for a programme that is too small to matter changes nothing.

**Confidence: low.** We contribute one input to a system with many.

### [4] Higher value added per worker, durable formal employment

**Assumption:** Firms crossing those thresholds produce the outcomes that matter for
structural transformation.

This is the background note's argument rather than ours, and we take it as given. The
honest position is that this project's contribution to [4] is unmeasurable in isolation
and should never be claimed.

**Confidence: not ours to assess.**

## What we will actually measure

Only what we can observe, mapped to the links above:

| Link | Measurable | Method |
|---|---|---|
| [1] | Completion; decision distribution; whether players converge on a single line | Behavioural record |
| [A] | Learner-stated intention change, pre/post; specific constraint named | In-product prompts |
| [A] | Real behaviour change | Partner follow-up. Self-reported — treat with the same scepticism as MbeleNaBiz |
| [B] | Whether staff say the record informed a decision | Partner interviews |
| [B] | Correlation with real firm outcomes | **Not measurable by us.** Needs an external study |
| [2]–[4] | — | Not attributable to this tool. Do not claim |

The gap between what we can measure and what we would like to claim is exactly the gap
the background note criticises other programmes for papering over. It stays visible.

## Falsifiers

Things that, if true, mean this project should change or stop:

- Learners' in-simulation behaviour tracks digital fluency more strongly than any
  business-relevant variable → the selection purpose is dead; keep the teaching tool
  and say so.
- Partner programmes cannot ingest the behavioural record in any form → [B] is blocked
  regardless of signal quality.
- Learners consistently report the scenarios do not resemble their situation → [1]
  fails at the base and everything above it is void.
- The tool measurably improves application quality without improving anything else →
  we have built the thing this project exists to avoid.
