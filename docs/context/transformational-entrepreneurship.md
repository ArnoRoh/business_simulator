# Transformational Entrepreneurship: Evidence, Critique, and Programme Design

## Purpose

This note summarises a discussion about the World Bank’s **MbeleNaBiz Kenyan business-plan competition** and the broader question of how development programmes can create genuinely transformational firms rather than merely enlarge livelihood microenterprises.

---

## 1. Starting Point

The World Bank article reports that business grants increased employment, sales, and profits among selected Kenyan firms. The most prominent result was that a **US$9,000 grant generated roughly two additional workers after about three years**, while a larger US$36,000 grant produced limited additional long-term benefit.

The core scepticism is that, based on operating experience in Tanzania:

- US$9,000 is often insufficient to complete the statutory, technical, and operational requirements of a properly formal business.
- The reported result may simply mean an owner-operated microbusiness became an owner-plus-two-casual-workers business.
- Those workers may be informal, poorly paid, part-time, or below minimum wage.
- This may improve household livelihoods without materially improving national productivity or structural transformation.

---

## 2. Main Methodological Concerns

The employment numbers appear to come primarily from entrepreneur surveys rather than independently verified payroll, tax, or social-security records.

The publicly available materials do not clearly establish:

- whether owners were counted;
- whether unpaid family labour was included;
- whether casual and seasonal workers were counted equally with permanent staff;
- whether headcount was converted to full-time equivalents;
- employee wages and hours;
- employment contracts or statutory compliance;
- worker retention;
- the reference period for employment;
- whether workers were verified physically or through payroll records.

The study therefore supports a narrower claim:

> Grant recipients reported employing more people at follow-up.

It does not clearly demonstrate:

> A precisely measured number of durable, formal, full-time, net new jobs.

Other concerns include:

- It measures gross employment at supported firms, not net economy-wide employment after competitor displacement.
- The final survey period spanned several months, raising potential seasonality issues.
- Attrition and treatment of closed businesses matter.
- Average results may be driven by a small number of firms.
- Failure to reject equal effects between US$9,000 and US$36,000 grants is not proof of equivalence.
- Extrapolating the US$9,000 result to a much larger programme assumes equally good marginal applicants exist.
- Cost-per-job calculations exclude programme administration and evaluation costs.
- Endpoint headcount is not the same as job-years or incremental worker income.

---

## 3. Cost-Effectiveness Concern

The article reports approximately **US$13.5 million in grants** and **1,313 additional jobs observed at the three-year endpoint**.

That equals approximately:

> US$10,300 in grant funding per observed endpoint job.

This excludes programme administration, screening, training, monitoring, and evaluation costs.

At Kenyan low-wage levels, that amount could plausibly pay several years—and in some cases around a decade—of direct wages for each reported job.

The key criticism is:

> Unless the programme created durable productive assets, capabilities, and future income beyond the wage bill, it may be an extremely expensive way to subsidise low-paid employment.

The larger-grant group reinforces this concern because recipients reportedly spent substantially on wages, rent, stock, and other operating costs, initially hired more workers, then contracted when the subsidy ran down.

---

## 4. Livelihood Versus Transformational Entrepreneurship

The relevant conceptual distinction is associated with **Antoinette Schoar**.

### 4.1 Livelihood or Subsistence Enterprise

A business primarily provides the owner with work and household income.

Typical characteristics:

- owner-operated;
- low barriers to entry;
- local commodity markets;
- little fixed capital;
- minimal organisational depth;
- family or casual labour;
- limited scalability;
- profits largely withdrawn for household consumption.

### 4.2 Transformational Enterprise

A business is intended and capable of building an organisation larger than the founder.

Typical characteristics:

- employs non-family workers;
- delegates management;
- invests in machinery, technology, standards, and systems;
- serves national, export, or business-to-business markets;
- has productive differentiation;
- reinvests for growth;
- creates formal or higher-quality jobs;
- develops supplier and value-chain linkages;
- can operate independently of the founder.

The distinction is not perfectly binary, but the literature suggests that ordinary microenterprises rarely become medium or large firms. Many future large firms look different relatively early in their life.

---

## 5. Broader Development Conclusion

Small firms are not useless. Supporting them may improve:

- household income;
- resilience;
- self-employment;
- consumption;
- local employment;
- asset accumulation.

That can be good poverty-alleviation or livelihood policy.

However, national development and structural transformation rely disproportionately on firms capable of:

- raising value added per worker;
- investing in technology;
- organising specialised labour;
- exporting;
- meeting standards;
- training workers;
- paying formal wages and taxes;
- creating supply-chain linkages;
- scaling beyond the owner.

The criticism is therefore not that livelihood interventions have no value. It is that they should not automatically be described as transformational job-creation policy.

## 6. Examples from the User’s Businesses

### 6.1 Upendo Honey / Third Man Ltd

Clearly transformational:

- approximately 130 employees;
- thousands of beekeeper suppliers;
- export markets;
- international certifications;
- formal processing infrastructure;
- organisational functions beyond the founder;
- substantial supplier and rural-income effects.

### 6.2 Tanganyika Blue

Transformational or high-potential:

- capital-intensive aquaculture;
- hatchery, cages, feed, and cold-chain requirements;
- formal employment;
- technical and regulatory complexity;
- potential development of an aquaculture ecosystem.

### 6.3 Dark Earth Carbon

Transformational in orientation, though still proving scale:

- industrial technology;
- engineering and O&M capability;
- carbon export revenues;
- repeatable facilities;
- MRV and certification systems;
- biomass supply-chain creation.

Its impact should not be measured only through employee headcount. Relevant measures include plants commissioned, tonnes processed, carbon removal, productivity, supplier income, and private capital mobilised.

---

## 7. What Business Competitions Have Shown

### 7.1 Nigeria YouWiN!

This is one of the strongest examples.

Features:

- grants averaging roughly US$50,000;
- selected, educated applicant pool;
- staged payments;
- employment and sales milestones;
- meaningful increases in firms reaching ten or more workers.

It provides evidence that large capital transfers to promising applicants can create larger firms.

However:

- judges and business-plan scores still predicted future winners poorly;
- the programme was expensive;
- long-term structural transformation remains less clear;
- firm selection remains difficult.

### 7.2 MbeleNaBiz

More useful for understanding:

- selection-process design;
- diminishing returns to grant size.

It does not clearly demonstrate that firms became formal, productive, scalable enterprises.

## 8. Interventions That Appear Most Promising

The strongest general principle is:

> Do not fund generic “business growth.” Remove a specific verified bottleneck in an existing high-potential firm.

### 8.1 Customer Creation and Procurement

- Give firms real purchase orders, not only grants.
- Use government, donor, and large-company procurement to qualify new suppliers.
- Demand validates the firm more effectively than a pitch.

### 8.2 Capability-Jump Funding

Finance a specific:

- machine;
- certification;
- pilot;
- laboratory test;
- technical manager;
- market-entry requirement.

Avoid using grants mainly for payroll, rent, and inventory.

### 8.3 Sector-Specific Group Consulting

Work with existing firms of meaningful scale.

Focus on:

- production;
- quality;
- costing;
- maintenance;
- sales systems;
- delegation.

Evidence from Colombian manufacturers suggests strong long-run effects.

### 8.4 Fractional Specialist Management

Subsidise experienced:

- CFOs;
- production engineers;
- export managers;
- quality specialists.

Transformational growth often fails because the founder cannot build an organisation beyond themselves.

### 8.5 Staged Funding

- Small initial experiments.
- Larger support only after customer validation and demonstrated execution.
- Treat the programme as a portfolio rather than trying to identify winners from applications.

### 8.6 Purchase-Order and Receivables Finance

Help firms finance confirmed orders using:

- guarantees;
- invoice discounting;
- escrow;
- structured working capital.

### 8.7 Shared Compliance and Technical Infrastructure

Provide access to:

- laboratories;
- calibration;
- certification;
- cold storage;
- technical services.

Avoid forcing each startup to recreate the same expensive compliance capability.

---

## 9. High-Volume Seeding With Tiny Capital

Tiny capital cannot build an industrial company. It can buy **information, proof, and optionality**.

A possible portfolio model:

- hundreds of US$500 commercial-discovery experiments;
- fewer US$2,500 paid trials;
- perhaps 20 US$10,000 first commercial batches;
- a handful of US$25,000 scale-preparation packages;
- private or customer-backed capital for actual scale-up.

The public programme should fund:

- prototypes;
- paid customer trials;
- engineering tests;
- samples;
- certification gap assessments;
- founder living support during experimentation;
- access to rented machinery or test facilities.

The programme should not require full incorporation and regulatory compliance at the first experimentation stage. Early teams could operate under a programme umbrella until commercial validation is achieved.

---

## 10. Novel or Under-Tested Programme Designs

### 10.1 Reverse Business-Plan Competitions

Large buyers publish real commercial problems or import-substitution needs. Teams compete to deliver a working solution. The prize is a paid trial or purchase order.

### 10.2 One-Bottleneck Auction

Existing firms state:

> “We can increase annual value added from X to Y if this specific constraint is removed.”

Programme staff verify the claim, estimate additionality, and fund the highest expected value-added per public dollar.

### 10.3 Recoverable Grants

Early support is repaid only if the firm reaches revenue or profitability thresholds. Recovered money funds later cohorts.

### 10.4 Supplier Spawning Around Anchor Firms

Successful firms identify missing suppliers or services. Experienced staff or contractors are supported to spin out, backed by an initial customer commitment from the anchor company.

### 10.5 Acquisition Entrepreneurship

Finance capable operators to acquire under-managed, retiring-owner, or distressed firms rather than starting greenfield businesses.

### 10.6 National Fractional-Management Corps

Place experienced operators and diaspora specialists into 10–100 employee firms for six to eighteen months, with co-payment and performance-based compensation.

## 11. Traits Associated With Transformational Founders

No reliable personality profile exists. Founder prediction remains weak.

The strongest indicators are:

- deep relevant industry experience;
- prior work in larger or formal organisations;
- evidence of execution under real constraints;
- desire to build an organisation, not merely own a job;
- willingness to delegate and share control;
- ability to recruit competent people;
- rapid learning and willingness to update assumptions;
- strong commercial realism;
- ability to leverage small resources into customers, supplier credit, and partnerships;
- high ambition combined with disciplined risk management.

Weak indicators include:

- youth;
- charisma;
- pitch quality;
- generic passion;
- hardship narratives;
- business degrees;
- job promises in applications;
- generic personality or “grit” questionnaires.

The best selection method is therefore:

> Use founder traits to improve the initial pool, then use real commercial behaviour to select.

A six- to twelve-week execution test should require candidates to:

- secure a paid trial or customer commitment;
- deliver a sample or service;
- establish credible unit economics;
- recruit a capable collaborator;
- maintain proper records;
- respond intelligently when assumptions change.

---

## 12. Central Conclusion

Development programmes should stop treating every microenterprise as an underfunded future medium-sized company.

A better strategy is:

> Source experienced operators and real market problems, fund many cheap experiments, select using observable execution, remove specific bottlenecks, and use customers and private capital to finance scale.

The key outcome should not be “businesses trained” or “jobs reported.”

It should be:

- firms crossing meaningful organisational thresholds;
- higher value added per worker;
- formal and sustained employment;
- machinery and productive capability;
- national or export market entry;
- private capital mobilisation;
- supplier development;
- durable movement from small to medium scale.
